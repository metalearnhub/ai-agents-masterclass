Most first AI agent projects do not fail at the technology. They fail before it: a vague goal ("let's add AI"), a scope that grows every week, no agreed definition of done, and a launch decided by enthusiasm instead of evidence. Gartner expects more than 40% of agentic AI projects to be canceled by the end of 2027, citing rising costs, unclear business value and weak risk controls.

This guide is the project plan I would want in hand before anyone builds. It is written for business owners, operations leads and the agencies that build agents for them, and it covers one job, one channel and one owner: scope, timeline, acceptance criteria and the launch decision.

1. Why first AI agent projects stall

Gartner's June 2025 prediction names three causes: costs that grow faster than expected, business value that was never defined, and risk controls that were not in place. It also warned about "agent washing": of the thousands of vendors claiming agentic AI, Gartner estimated only about 130 offer real agentic features. A project plan answers all three causes up front, in writing: what value, at what cost, under which controls.

The plan also protects you from building too much. Anthropic's guidance on building agents makes the same point: start with the simplest solution that works and add agentic complexity only when the task needs it. Many "agent" projects are better served by a fixed workflow or a chatbot. If you are not sure which you need, decide that first with AI agent vs chatbot vs automation.

2. Scope one job, on one page

A first agent should do one job well, on one channel, for one group of users. "Answer customer questions" is not a job. "Answer pricing and availability questions on Messenger and book a call for qualified leads" is. Write the scope before you choose any tool, and keep it to one page so everyone reads it.

AI AGENT SCOPE (one page)
Job:            [the one task, in one sentence]
Channel:        [Messenger / website chat / phone / email]
Users:          [who talks to it]
In scope:       [3-5 things it must do]
Out of scope:   [what it must NOT do, and where those go]
Knowledge:      [sources it may answer from, and who keeps them current]
Systems:        [calendar, CRM, inventory; read-only or write]
Handoff:        [when it hands to a person, and to whom]
Success metric: [one number that proves value, with today's baseline]
Owner:          [one named person who decides and signs off]
Budget:         [setup, monthly platform and usage, review time]

The two lines people skip are the ones that matter most. Out of scope stops the project growing every week. The baseline for the success metric is what lets you prove value later: if you do not know your response time or booking rate today, measure it before you build. The process work behind this step is in Don't Automate Chaos.

3. The six-week timeline

For a single-job agent on one channel, six weeks is a realistic plan when the knowledge already exists and the systems have working connections. Treat it as a typical plan, not a promise: messy data or a new integration adds time, and a simple FAQ agent can take less.

4. Write acceptance criteria before you build

Acceptance criteria are the pass lines the agent must meet before it goes live. Writing them in week 1, before anyone builds, turns "it seems fine" into a decision you can defend. Each criterion needs a target and a way to measure it. The targets below are examples to set with your team, not industry benchmarks.

CriterionExample targetHow to measure
Answer accuracyCorrect on at least 18 of 20 real past questionsTest set built from your own inbox
Stays in scopeDeclines or hands off every out-of-scope question10 off-topic and tricky questions
No made-up factsZero invented prices, policies or promisesAsk about things that are not in the knowledge
Handoff worksEvery handoff reaches the named person with a summaryTrigger each handoff rule once
Actions are correctEvery booking or record update matches the source systemBook, change and cancel; check the calendar or CRM
SpeedFirst reply within the time you promised customersTimed test messages on the live channel
PrivacyCollects only the data the job needsReview the questions and stored fields

Write the important cases as plain scenarios so the owner and the builder read them the same way:

Given a customer asks the price of [service on the price list]
When the agent answers
Then it states the listed price and offers the next step

Given a customer asks about something not in the knowledge
When the agent answers
Then it says it will check and hands off, with no guess

Given a customer asks for a person
When the agent replies
Then it hands off to [name] and tells the customer when to expect a reply
Dijae's rule

If you cannot write the pass line before the build, you are not ready to build. Agree on what "done" means first; the tool choice comes after.

The full testing method, with a test set you can adapt, is in how to test an AI chatbot.

5. Plan the risk and the budget

You do not need an enterprise framework for a first agent, but borrowing its shape helps. The NIST AI Risk Management Framework, a voluntary framework published in January 2023, organizes the work into four functions. Here is what each means for a small first project:

NIST functionWhat it means for your first agent
GovernOne named owner who can pause the agent and approves every change
MapA short list of what could go wrong: wrong prices, wrong bookings, data shared, an upset customer stuck with a bot
MeasureThe acceptance criteria and the weekly review numbers
ManageGuardrails, handoff rules and a way to switch the agent off quickly
Keep version 1 read-only where you can

Let the first agent read your calendar, CRM or inventory, and write only where the job needs it, such as creating a booking. Leave refunds, payments, deletions and bulk updates to people until the agent has a track record. The layers of protection are in AI agent guardrails.

For the budget, list four lines: setup (build time), the platform's monthly plan, AI model usage per conversation, and the weekly review time someone on your team will spend. The per-reply math is in how much an AI chatbot really costs, and choosing a model tier is in choosing the right AI model for a business agent.

6. Launch with a go/no-go check, then review every week

Launch is a decision, not a date. On the planned launch day, the owner answers five questions. If any answer is no, fix that item first and check again.

Start the soft launch on a slice you can watch: one channel, after-hours only, or one listing or service. Read every conversation in the first week. From then on, hold a 30-minute weekly review: read a sample of conversations, fix the knowledge and rules, and track the success metric against the baseline. The routine is in AI agent monitoring after launch.

How you will know the project worked: after 30 days, the success metric has moved from the baseline in the right direction, every acceptance criterion still passes on a re-run of the test set, and the owner can name the top three fixes made from the weekly reviews.

An honest limitation: this plan is built for a first, single-job agent. It does not cover multi-agent systems, regulated industries with formal model approval, or large integrations with legacy systems, which need longer timelines and more formal governance. And a good plan cannot rescue a job that was never worth automating; that call belongs in week 1.

Frequently asked questions

How long does it take to build a first AI agent?

For one job on one channel, with the knowledge already written and working system connections, about six weeks is a realistic plan: one week to scope, one for knowledge, two to build, one to test and one to launch. Messy data or new integrations add time.

What should be in an AI agent project scope?

The one job, the channel, the users, what is in and out of scope, the knowledge sources, the systems it can read or write, the handoff rule, one success metric with a baseline, a named owner and the budget.

What are acceptance criteria for an AI agent?

They are pass lines the agent must meet before launch, each with a target and a way to measure it: answer accuracy on real past questions, staying in scope, no invented facts, working handoffs, correct actions, speed and privacy.

Why do AI agent projects fail?

Gartner points to rising costs, unclear business value and inadequate risk controls. A one-page scope, a baseline metric, acceptance criteria and a named owner address all three before the build starts.
