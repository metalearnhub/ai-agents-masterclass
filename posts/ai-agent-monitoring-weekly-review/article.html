Launch day is when an AI agent stops being tested by you and starts being tested by your customers. They ask questions you never wrote down, prices and policies change under it, and the model behind it gets updated by its provider. An agent that passed every test in week one can be quietly wrong by week six, and nobody notices until a customer complains.

1. Why agents drift after launch

Most agent failures after launch are not caused by anyone breaking the agent. The world around it moves:

  • Your business changes. A new price, a new branch, a paused promo or a new delivery area. If the knowledge files are not updated the same day, the agent keeps quoting the old version.
  • Customers change. Real people ask in ways your test set never covered, mix several questions into one message, and bring up topics you never planned for.
  • The platform changes. Model providers update models, and chatbot platforms ship new features and defaults. The same instructions can produce slightly different behavior after an update.

So why does this matter? Because none of these show up as an error message. The agent keeps replying, fluently and confidently. The only way to see drift is to look at what it actually says to real customers, on a schedule.

The NIST AI Risk Management Framework makes the same point in its MANAGE function: post-deployment monitoring plans should be implemented, including ways to capture and evaluate input from users, incident response and change management. That is written for any AI system. For a business chatbot it becomes one routine you run every week.

Dijae's rule

Read real conversations every week. Numbers tell you that something changed; conversations tell you why. An agent nobody reads is an agent nobody manages.

2. The five signals to watch

You do not need a large dashboard. Five numbers, tracked the same way every week, catch most problems early. Write down this week's value next to last week's.

SignalHow to count itWhat a rise usually means
Handoff rateConversations passed to a person, divided by all conversationsA new question type, or the agent is unsure more often
Unanswered or droppedConversations that ended with no useful reply or no next stepGaps in knowledge, or a broken flow or tool
CorrectionsReplies your team had to correct or follow up onWrong or outdated facts in the answers
ComplaintsNegative messages about the agent or the answerTone, trapped customers, or promises you cannot keep
OutcomeBookings, orders or qualified leads from agent conversationsA drop means something upstream is leaking

The first four are early warnings; the fifth is the business result. If you already track the metrics in the 5 chatbot ROI metrics, reuse them here so the weekly review and the ROI view tell the same story.

3. The 30-minute weekly review

Put it in the calendar for the same day and time every week, with one named owner. The routine is a loop: sample, score, find the cause, fix one thing, and retest.

  1. Pull (5 minutes). Write down the five signals for the week and export or open the conversations you will read (see the next section).
  2. Score (15 minutes). Read each sampled conversation and score it with the scorecard below. Mark every failure.
  3. Find the cause (5 minutes). Group the failures by cause: instructions, knowledge or tools.
  4. Fix one thing (5 minutes to plan). Pick the cause behind the most failures and fix that one, today or as this week's task.
  5. Retest and log. Re-run your test set after the fix, add each new failure as a new test case, and write one line in a change log: date, what changed, why, result.

The test set is the one you built before launch. If you do not have one yet, the method is in how to test an AI chatbot before it talks to your customers.

4. How many conversations to read

You cannot read everything, and you do not need to. A practical weekly sample for a small or mid-sized business:

  • Every handoff and every conversation flagged by the team or a complaint.
  • Every conversation that ended with no reply or no next step.
  • A random sample of ordinary conversations, enough that you read at least 20 to 30 in total each week.

The random part matters. Problems in quiet conversations, such as a slightly wrong delivery fee, never trigger a handoff or a complaint, and you only find them by reading at random.

Larger teams automate this. State Farm's engineering team has described a scheduled job that pulls a statistically sized sample of conversation logs and scores them with a language model as a judge, while subject-matter experts review the reports and periodically compare their own scores with the judge's. The principle scales down: sample on a schedule, score consistently, and keep humans checking the scorer.

5. Score conversations the same way every time

If two people review the same conversation and reach different verdicts, your trend lines mean nothing. Use one short scorecard for every conversation, every week.

WEEKLY CONVERSATION SCORECARD
Conversation ID: ____    Date: ____    Channel: ____

1. ACCURATE      Every fact (price, policy, hours, stock) matches
                 the current files.                      Yes / No
2. GROUNDED      No invented details, no guesses presented
                 as facts.                               Yes / No
3. GUARDRAILS    Stayed in scope; no promises, discounts or
                 advice it was not allowed to give.      Yes / No
4. HANDOFF       Handed off when it should, and not when it
                 should not; summary included.           Yes / No
5. OUTCOME       The customer got an answer or a clear next
                 step (booking, order, person).          Yes / No

If any answer is No:
Cause:  Instructions / Knowledge / Tools / Other
Note:   what it said vs. what it should have said

Each line maps to a layer of the agent. Accuracy and grounding test the knowledge; guardrails and handoff test the instructions and limits described in guardrails for business chatbots; outcome tests whether the whole thing earns its keep.

If you use a language model to pre-score conversations, treat it as a first pass, not a verdict. The State Farm team noted that their judge could be too generous, miss nuance, or produce convincing but wrong explanations, which is why their experts still review and compare. Spot-check the judge every week with a few conversations you score yourself.

6. Route every failure to its cause

A failure is a symptom. Fixing it in the wrong place adds another rule to an already long prompt and changes nothing. Almost every failure traces back to one of three places.

SymptomLikely causeFix
Quotes last month's priceKnowledgeUpdate the price file the day prices change
Two answers to the same questionKnowledgeRemove the duplicate; keep one home per fact
Answers a topic it should declineInstructionsTighten the scope guardrail and add a test case
Keeps a frustrated customer in the botInstructionsAdd the trigger to the handoff rules
Says a slot is free that is takenToolsCheck the calendar connection and permissions
Correct facts, but sounds roboticInstructionsRevise the voice section with real examples

The knowledge fixes follow the one-fact-one-home rule in how to ground an AI agent in your business knowledge. The instruction fixes follow the structure in how to write AI agent instructions for any business.

7. Change one thing, then retest

When five things change at once and the numbers move, you cannot tell which change helped and which one hurt. Change one thing per review, retest, and write it down.

Acceptance criteria for any change: the failure it targets no longer happens in the test set; every existing test case still passes; and the change is logged with the date and reason. If an existing test fails, roll the change back first and think second.

Over a month, the change log becomes the most useful document you own about your agent. When a customer asks why the agent said something odd last Tuesday, you can answer which version was running and what has changed since. The same discipline applies to the business process behind the agent, as covered in don't automate chaos.

8. When to review today

Some events cannot wait for the weekly slot. Run a focused review, and re-run the test set, the same day any of these happen:

  • A price, policy, promo or product change goes live.
  • Your chatbot platform or model provider announces a model or feature update.
  • A complaint mentions the agent, or the handoff rate jumps.
  • You add a new channel, tool or integration.
  • A campaign or ad sends a new kind of customer to the chat.

The metric that tells you the routine works: the share of sampled conversations with any "No" on the scorecard. It should fall over the first month and then stay low. If it rises two weeks in a row, the agent is drifting faster than you are fixing it, and the review needs more time or a second person.

An honest limitation: a weekly sample catches patterns, not every individual mistake. A rare but costly error can slip through a random sample for weeks. That is why the high-risk topics, money, promises and sensitive data, belong behind guardrails and human approval, not behind the review alone.

Frequently asked questions

How often should I review my AI chatbot after launch?

Every week, at a fixed time, with one named owner. Review immediately when prices, policies, tools or the underlying model change, or when complaints or handoffs suddenly rise.

How many conversations should I read each week?

Read every handoff, every complaint and every conversation that ended without a reply, plus a random sample, so you read at least 20 to 30 in total. The random sample catches quiet errors that never trigger a complaint.

Can an AI score my chatbot's conversations for me?

Yes, as a first pass. A language model can pre-score conversations against your scorecard, but it can be too generous or miss nuance, so a person should still review the results and spot-check the scores every week.

What should I do when the agent gives a wrong answer?

Decide whether the cause is the instructions, the knowledge files or a tool, fix it in that one place, add the case to your test set, retest everything and log the change.
